Why most ad blockers quietly stop working

The pattern is always the same: an extension ships, a site updates its delivery, and the lists that made the extension useful go stale within a week. What's left is a badge in the toolbar that claims protection while the page loads four more network calls than it did last month.

Filter lists are the whole product

An ad blocker is a text file and a matcher. Everything else — the interface, the icon, the settings pane — is decoration around those two things. When the list stops being maintained, the product is gone even if the shell still installs.

That's why the useful question is never "which blocker" but "who maintains the list, and how fast do they ship on a Friday".

Detection is cheaper than blocking

Sites learned to probe for the side effects of blocking rather than the blocking itself: a bait element with a tell-tale height, a script tag whose `onerror` fires, a fetch to a known tracker host. Beat one probe and another replaces it, usually within the same quarter.

Nothing about this arms race is technical any more. It's scheduling.

What actually helps

DNS-level filtering catches the requests that never needed to reach a browser, which removes most of the surface before a page can probe it. Beyond that, keep the list count low, update daily, and accept that a handful of sites will keep winning.

Whitelisting the ones that behave is cheaper than fighting them.
